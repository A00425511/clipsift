    How far can you see?
    How to judge it
    • Pole and tape: how far can you still clearly read the numbers?
    • Also check how far you can make out seabed features. The pole stays visible further than a fish would (Baker 2022).
    • The distance at which you can identify a fish is shorter than the distance you can see a target: about 50 to 70% of a Secchi reading (DFO note in the protocol's comments).
    • Visibility limits what can be seen, so a low count in poor visibility doesn't mean few fish. Keep this in mind when comparing videos.

                What this tool does

                ClipSift walks you through Coastal Action's video analysis protocol. It sets the analysis window, cuts every count point out of the video, works out the results and writes the report. You judge visibility, name the species and decide what counts.

                The steps for each video

                1. Visibility: rate it once for the video. 2 to 3 people each do this on their own.
                2. Watch window: watch in real time and note which species are present, and whether juveniles are. No counting yet.
                3. Count points: at each point, play the clip in the picture, mark what you are sure of, check the numbers and save. Count only in the highlighted second. Use 0.25x to slow it down.
                4. Quality check: a second person opens the video under their own name and counts the held frames blind. ClipSift compares the two and shows how close they are. The numbers never hold up a video.
                5. Report: print it or save it as a PDF.
                6. Check model flags: optional. Check a mix of flagged items, which can help train a better detector later.

                Opening a video

                On the home screen, drop a file or choose one. Check the details, press Start, and ClipSift cuts out the count points. It opens the video when it's ready, and asks who is working on it so counts are saved under the right name.

                The counting rules, in brief

                • Count only what is inside the one designated second, which starts at the count point. A fish that appears within about half a second either side at 0.25x may count. Past that, add a note instead.
                • Another fish may count only if you are completely sure it is a different fish. The rule exists to avoid counting one fish twice.
                • Count an animal only if two of these four tell it apart: shape, anatomical features, colour or flashes, swimming or movement.
                • When unsure, leave it out. Counting too few is better than counting too many.
                • Movement alone is not counted. In a school, count only the fish you can confirm, and tick the note for the rest.
                • Counts are a relative index, not how many animals are really there.
